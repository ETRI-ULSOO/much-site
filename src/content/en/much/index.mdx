## Summary in One Paragraph

The digital records that museums and galleries created for their artifacts grew larger every year, but each institution made them differently, so data created in one place was hard to reuse elsewhere. MUCH (Multi-purpose Universal Cultural Heritage) addressed this in two ways: by setting standards for the procedures and quality used to create digital data, and by building a platform that connected that data so it could be drawn on for multiple purposes.

## Why It Was Needed

### Records Kept Growing, but Reuse Was Difficult

Even when photographing the same artifact, institutions differed in resolution, lighting, file format, and metadata fields. As a result, every new exhibition or set of educational materials required reshooting from scratch.

### Relationships Between Artifacts Were Not Captured in the Data

Information describing individual artifacts existed, but data that recorded which period an artifact belonged to or which artifacts shared a lineage — in a form a machine could follow — was rare.

### Content Was Used Once and Then Set Aside

High-resolution material produced for an exhibition sat in storage once the exhibition ended, because it was not in a form that could be recombined for other purposes.

## What Was Built

### Data Fabric Architecture

The project designed an architecture that let cultural heritage data scattered across different locations be used together without collecting it all in one place. Instead of moving data, it standardized a way of pointing to data.

### Knowledge Graph Platform

Relationships among artifacts, periods, materials, techniques, and people were linked in a graph. It is built on CIDOC CRM, an international standard, so it can also connect with data from other institutions.

### AI-Based Visualization

The project developed ways to display the relationship graph so people can follow it visually. It offers four paths together: browsing a hierarchical structure, searching by keyword, paging through a catalog, and querying directly.

### Heritage-Specific Service Technology

Features were built to meet needs specific to the cultural heritage field, including automatic image classification, guidance for visitor routes, and displaying restored versions of damaged areas.

### Building Composable Datasets

12,131 items of data were created, stored with their components separated so that, once made, they could be used for multiple purposes. The contractual target was 4,500 items.

### Generative AI Applications

The project tested methods for generating new content from the data already created.

## Research Goals

The goal was to establish standards for creating digital cultural heritage data and to develop an intelligent sharing platform that let multiple fields share data meeting those standards. There were three specific objectives.

1. Enact a domestic standard for creating and assuring the quality of digital cultural heritage assets.
2. Develop a knowledge graph capturing relationships between artifacts and a platform for exploring it.
3. Verify real-world use through a demonstration at the National Museum of Korea.

### Technical Aspect

The procedure for creating cultural heritage data was organized into a standard, so data of the same quality can be obtained regardless of which institution creates it. The demonstration at the National Museum of Korea confirmed that the procedure works in practice.

### Cultural Aspect

As artifacts that once stayed inside museums moved onto large public screens and digital exhibits, people who do not visit exhibition halls also gained access to cultural heritage.

### Industrial Aspect

Data was prepared in a form that content producers can use directly, without having to reshoot cultural heritage material themselves.
